3  Looking for Patterns in More than One Variable

Many of the interesting and useful patterns out there are those that involve more than one variable. These allow us to posit a relationship based on causal mechanisms, or develop a useful metric for predicting the state of one variable from another variable.

When we look at two variables together, it’s called bivariate analysis, and when we look at more than two we usually use the term multivariate analysis.

Categorical and numerical variables

Earlier, we saw how we can use facets to break up a plot based on a grouping category. For example, we can evaluate the distribution of weights by sex:

ggplot(olympics,aes(x=Weight)) +
  geom_histogram(na.rm=TRUE,binwidth = 10) +
  facet_wrap(vars(Sex))

It we wanted to do this across two grouping categories, we’d want to create a matrix or grid of plots, with one category going vertically and the other horizontally. We can do this with the facet_grid function, for example between sex and whether the Olympics are taking place in summer or winter:

ggplot(olympics,aes(x=Weight)) +
  geom_histogram(na.rm=TRUE,binwidth = 10) +
  facet_grid(cols=vars(Sex),rows=vars(Season))

Of course, another way to look at combinations of categorical and numerical variables is to use boxplots. Like histograms, boxplots show the center, spread, and shape of distributions, but these are most useful when comparing more than one group. These usually have four main components:

  • Center line The central line of the boxplot is the median value.

  • Box The “box” part of the box is an indication of the interquartile range: the values between the 25% and 75% quartiles. Half of our data falls within the range defined by the bounds of the box.

  • Whiskers The whiskers are the lines at either end of the boxplot. These extend to 10% and 90%, respectively.

  • Outliers Boxplots use individual points to visualize outliers, which is anything that falls beyond the reach of the whiskers.

Lots of variables to look at here, including nominal (city, type), interval (latitude, longitude), and ratio (beds, sqft, price). To get a quick sense of what things are looking like, we can use a boxplot to examine the relationship between a nominal variable (type) and a ratio variable (price):

ggplot(data=Sacramento,aes(x=type,y=price)) +   
  geom_boxplot()

First, there’s a good deal of overlap between these different categories. However, the mean values are greater for multifamily and residential-type housing than they are for condominiums. Finally, there are a fair number of outliers in the residential-type, indicating a positive skew in the data. By viewing these boxplots side-by-side, we’re turning our univariate view of price into a bivariate view by type.

These are metrics on black-lipped abalone from Tasmania, used to evaluate the relationship between physical size and age (as determined by growth rings). This data has a number of ratio values, but it’s not immediately what the some of the units are just by looking: given the average length of an abalone shell (80-130mm), they don’t really work for most of the common kinds of measurement units we might encounter (millimeters, centimeters, inches).

This is the kind of problem that metadata are good for. The original data were actually re-scaled from millimeters and grams to use in a machine-learning exercise by dividing them all by 200. We could do a wholesale conversion of these values if we wanted to use this data for a more intensive analysis, but since since we’re just exploring we’ll just do this when we do our aesthetic mapping.

Patterns that we might be looking for in the shape of a scatterplot are:

  • Direction An increase in one variable generally corresponds with a change, either increase (positive direction) or decrease (negative direction), in the other.

  • Form The change seems to follow a line (linear) or a curve (curvilinear).

  • Strength The slope of a change is either steep (strong) or shallow (weak).

  • Scatter If a line were drawn through the middle of the points along its primary direction, this indicates how close the points are to that line

Let’s have a look at the abalone data to see what we mean. First, we’ll look at the relationship between shell diameter and the number of growth rings:

ggplot(data=abalone,aes(x=diameter*200,y=rings)) +
  geom_point() +
  labs(x="Diameter (mm)",y="Number of Rings")

What do you notice that’s a little strange about this data? It seems to be organized in horizontal lines. Both of our values (diameter and number of rings) are ratio data, but one of them (number of rings) is an integer and therefore discrete. This means that gaps exist between our values, creating the visual effect of horizontal lines.

What else do we see about this relationship? It seems as though the number of rings increases with increasing diameter, suggesting a positive direction. We can visualize this more clearly by adding a line that tries to minimize the distance between itself and all the points using the geom_smooth function:

ggplot(data=abalone,aes(x=diameter*200,y=rings)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth() +
  labs(x="Diameter (mm)",y="Number of Rings")
`geom_smooth()` using method = 'gam' and formula = 'y ~ s(x, bs = "cs")'

The geom_smooth function runs a series of calculations that draws a line between points minimize the error (distance) to all other points in a given window of values. It then draws a line along with a shaded envelope indicating a confidence interval. The line itself is the best estimate the smoothing function produced, while the confidence interval provides the range produced by 95% of the estimates closest to the best estimate. This gives us a summary of how the number of rings relates to the diameter of the abalone.

Now let’s look at the relationship between diameter and whole weight of the shellfish:

ggplot(data=abalone,aes(x=diameter,y=weight.whole)) +
  geom_point() +
  labs(x="Diameter (mm)",y="Whole Weight (g)")

In this case, the weight increases along with the diameter, but the rate of increase is not consistent; it seems to increase with diameter. On a basic level, we could say that there is a curvilinear relationship here, which we can illustrate again with a smoothed line:

ggplot(data=abalone,aes(x=diameter*200,y=weight.whole*200)) + geom_point() +
  geom_smooth() +
  labs(x="Diameter (mm)",y="Whole Weight (g)")
`geom_smooth()` using method = 'gam' and formula = 'y ~ s(x, bs = "cs")'

Numerical descriptions of relationships

In the last section, we talked about ways we can describe distributions using numerical summaries like mean and standard deviation. A common metric for describing the relationship between two variables is called the correlation coefficient, sometimes also referred to as r in statistics contexts1.

To get a sense of this, let’s look at the abalone data again, with diameter and length as the x and y variables.

ggplot(data=abalone,aes(x=diameter*200,y=length*200)) + geom_point() +
  labs(x="Diameter (mm)",y="Length (mm)")

This data is not just linear, it’s also not very scattered: we can see that plotting it creates a diagonal line from bottom left to top right, with not a lot of spread around the central line.

We can get the correlation coefficient using the cor function (note: we’re using base R here, so we need to use the $ symbol to access columns):

cor(abalone$diameter*200,abalone$length*200)
[1] 0.9868116

What does this number mean? The correlation coefficient is expressed as a value between -1 and 1. Whether the value is positive or negative is indicating whether the relationship is positive (y goes up as x goes up) or negative (y goes down as x goes up), while distance from zero is indicating how closely that value holds to the line. You can see some examples here:

Let’s look at another example, this time using the number of rings and the shucked weight.

ggplot(data=abalone,aes(x=rings,y=weight.shucked*200)) + geom_point() +
  labs(x="Number of Rings",y="Shucked Weight (g)")

This still increases, but the linear nature of the relationship between number of rings and shucked weight is not nearly as clear, particularly as the number of growth rings goes up. This should produce a lower correlation coefficient, and when we use cor, we find that in fact it does.

cor(abalone$rings,abalone$weight.shucked*200)
[1] 0.4208837

Metrics like the correlation coefficient provide a convenient way to examine and compare distributions of values. At the same time, it’s always a good idea to combine these calculations with visualizations, particularly in instances where numerical summary values are not extreme.

Comparing correlations across multiple quantitative variables

If we have several numerical values, we can individually plot each combination that interests us, but what we may actually want to do is find a way to quickly see the range of relationships between these variables.

One way to do this would be to create a correlation matrix, which will. First, we need to remove the categorical variables or any we’re not interested in using here. in the case of the abalone dataset, this would be the first two columns (which give the ID number and species). We can do that using the following code:

abaloneNums<-abalone[,-c(1:2)]

This is a subsetting operation, which we can tell from the use of square brackets. There is a comma, and everything in this operation is happening on the right of the comma, which means we’re subsetting column-wise. There is a hyphen (actually a minus symbol), indicating that we intend to subtract something. That something is a vector of the numbers 1 through 2. This says that we want to subtract columns 1 through 2 from the abalone dataset.

The next t thing we need to compare all of these variables is a correlation matrix, which will give the correlation coefficient for all our variable pairs. We can create one of these by giving our tibble of numerical values to the cor function:

abaloneCor<-cor(abaloneNums)

Each row and column in this matrix is a variable in our dataset, so each value is the correlation coefficient from a pair of variables. We can see from this that all of them are positively correlated. This is OK, but not especially intuitive to look at.

We can visually inspect these relationships using a correlation plot, which we can access by using the corrplot package (run the install.packages line without the comment on your end):

#install.packages("corrplot")
library(corrplot)
corrplot 0.95 loaded

Once you have the corrplot package, you can create a correlation plot. Let’s look at one using the mtcars dataset first:

carCor<-cor(mtcars)
corrplot(carCor,method="ellipse")

Each ellipse in this graph is characterizing the relationship between variable pairs. Ellipses that are more elongated indicate a directional relationship, while those that are more circular indicate a weaker or non-existent relationship. The direction of the ellipse and the color are reflecting whether the correlation is positive or negative.

This dataset has a range of different kinds of relationships. What about our abalone dataset?

corrplot(abaloneCor, method = 'ellipse')

What we can see here is that, on the whole, the distance and weight variables are highly correlated with one another, which we would expect. Number of rings, on the other hand, shows generally positive relationships with the other variables but the relationship is not nearly as strong.

Three or more variables where at least two are numerical

Depending on the way we are going to do it, exploring three or more variables at a time can be relatively easy, but also present substantial challenges. This is because our visualizations, being situated on a screen or printed document, are constrained to 2-dimensional representation. We need to find ways to work around this if we want to add more variables.

If we’re already visualizing two numerical variables in a scatterplot, adding variables can be done in a few ways. One would be by mapping it on to another aspect of the graph, like color, fill, or symbol shape. We’ve done this before, as in the example below:

ggplot(data=abalone,aes(x=diameter*200,y=weight.whole*200,color=sex)) + geom_point() +
  labs(x="Diameter (mm)",y="Whole Weight (g)",color="Sex")

Or for symbol shape:

ggplot(data=abalone,aes(x=diameter*200,y=weight.whole*200,shape=sex)) + geom_point() +
  labs(x="Diameter (mm)",y="Whole Weight (g)",shape="Sex")

If we want to represent 3 numerical variables, we can try to do this similarly using a gradient:

ggplot(data=abalone,aes(x=diameter*200,y=weight.whole*200,color=length*200)) + geom_point() +
  labs(x="Diameter (mm)",y="Whole Weight (g)",color="Length (mm)")

These each have their pluses and minuses. While they are reasonable simple to generate, because of the degree of overlap, it can be hard to see what pattern exists, if any. We may need to modify things like size and transparency to get a better view of the patterning.

Sometimes, though, we may truly want to explore multiple variables in a three-dimensional coordinate space. This can be done in ggplot2 by adding the ggcube package:

#install.packages("ggcube")
library(ggcube)

To make a 3D scatterplot, in addition to mapping values to x and y position, we also map them on to a z position. Then we add the function coord_3d to project them in a 3D space:

ggplot(data=abalone,aes(x=diameter*200,y=weight.whole*200,z=length*200)) + 
  geom_point() +
  coord_3d() +
  labs(x="Diameter (mm)",y="Whole Weight (g)",z="Length (mm)")

This looks good, but we may want to modify the angle we’re looking at by adding arguments for roll, yaw, and pitch:

ggplot(data=abalone,aes(x=diameter*200,y=weight.whole*200,z=length*200)) + 
  geom_point() +
  coord_3d(roll=-45, yaw=-45 , pitch = 45) +
  labs(x="Diameter (mm)",y="Whole Weight (g)",z="Length (mm)")

This is OK, but kind of a clunky way to explore a 3D scatterplot. R has other packages that will let us create these kinds of plots with more interactivity. One of these is plotly:

#install.packages("plotly")
library(plotly)

The plotly R package is derived from a broader set of Plotly visualization tools, and provides functions for creating interactive, 3D plots directly from the R interface.

The function plot_ly is similar to ggplot, but with slightly different syntax. In particular, it forgoes the aes function in the aesthetic mapping, uses the tilde (~) to indicate variable names, and uses type/mode arguments rather than layering a geometry (note: running this code may take a minute to produce a graph):

plot_ly(data = abalone, 
        x = ~diameter, 
        y = ~weight.whole, 
        z = ~rings,
        type='scatter3d',
        mode = 'markers')

We can make additional modifications to further update the graph with different appearance (like changing the size of points) or to introduce new variables (like mapping abalone sex to color):

plot_ly(data = abalone, 
        x = ~diameter, 
        y = ~weight.whole, 
        z = ~rings,
        color=~sex,
        size=1,
        type='scatter3d',
        mode = 'markers'
        )

Plotly has additional fine controls that can be used to modify the appearance of the graph. You can also export snapshots of the graph, or embed it in an HTML document for webviewing as you’re seeing here. There’s more information in the package’s help documentation, or you can follow a tutorial on it here.

This is just one of many graphics packages R has available to use, and is introduced here so you can get a sense of what is possible. You may decide that this kind of visualization would be helpful for your project and are welcome to investigate it further (and the teaching staff would be excited to help you do that!). However, most of our plotting needs will be working in 2D coordinate spaces, and for that ggplot2 has plenty of capabilities we have not yet explored.
